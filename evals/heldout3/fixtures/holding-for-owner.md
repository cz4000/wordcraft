# Holding for Owner

The lost property office at Corley Street station was a room you could walk past for years without seeing, and Aunt Ros said that was how it ought to be. "Nobody comes here on a good day," she told Ivo on his first morning. "If everyone knew where we were, it'd mean they'd lost too much."

She said it while unlocking the shutter with a key that hung from a shoelace round her neck, and Ivo, who was fourteen and had been sent there for the summer because his mum had started nights at the depot and didn't want him "rotting in the flat", decided this was the kind of thing adults said to make a small room sound important.

The room was small. It had a counter, a hatch, a kettle, a radio that only got one station, and behind a curtain, the shelves: umbrellas in a dustbin by the hundred, a wall of phones in plastic bags, a crate of single gloves that Ros refused to throw away because "the other one's out there somewhere, looking". There was a cello. There was a prosthetic leg with a sock still on it. There was a shoebox labelled TEETH that Ivo did not open.

Everything that came in went into the ledger. The station had a computer system, and Ros used it, but she also wrote every item by hand into a long green book with a cracked spine: date, item, where found, who handed it in, and a last column headed DISPOSAL, which was where you wrote what happened in the end. *Collected*, with a signature. Or *Sold*, after three months, at the auction in Dunford. Or *Destroyed*, for the phones nobody claimed and the sandwiches people kept handing in as if they were treasure.

"Why do both?" Ivo asked.

"Computers forget," said Ros.

"Computers literally don't forget. That's the whole point of them."

"Then they won't mind me checking."

His job was to sort and label, mostly, and to make the tea, and on the first Thursday he learned his other job, which was to be polite to Mr Abernathy.

Mr Abernathy came at eleven. He was tall and folded-up, in a jacket too warm for July, and he took his cap off at the hatch as if he were at a church door.

"Good morning," he said. "I wonder if you can help me. I left an umbrella on the 8.14 last week. A green one. It has a handle like a duck's head."

"Let me have a look for you," said Ros, and she went behind the curtain, and Ivo heard her not looking. She stood by the umbrella bin for the time it took to count to thirty, and then she came back and said, "Nothing yet, I'm afraid. Would you like a cup of tea while you're here?"

He would. He drank it standing at the hatch, talking about the weather and the price of a return to Dunford and a man he'd known called Pinder who had kept pigeons, and then he thanked her, put his cap back on, and went.

"He was here last Thursday too," said Ros, before Ivo could ask. "And the one before. Every Thursday since March."

"Did he lose it in March?"

"He lost it last week," said Ros. "Every week, he lost it last week."

---

By the third Thursday Ivo had decided it was stupid. He said so, which was a mistake.

"What's stupid?"

"Pretending. You don't even look. You just stand there."

"I know what's in the bin."

"So tell him. Tell him it's not here and it's not coming."

Ros put down the bag of phones she was labelling. She didn't get angry often, and when she did it came out quiet, as if she'd turned the volume down on herself to make room for it.

"And then what?"

"Then he'd stop coming."

"Yes," said Ros. "He would."

Ivo didn't have an answer to that, so he went and made the tea too strong, on purpose, and she drank it without saying anything, which was worse.

---

It was the ledgers that changed it. There were forty years of them on a high shelf at the back, green books fading toward brown as they got older, and in the slow hours of August Ros let him take them down. He told himself it was boredom. Really it was that the old ledgers were better than anything on his phone. 1979: a wedding dress, collected the next day, no signature, just a note in the margin in someone's neat hand: *She said she was glad to have the choice.* 1983: a live tortoise. 1988: a suitcase containing four hundred pounds in five-pound notes, handed in by a cleaner called Mrs Dee, collected by a man who signed with an X.

He was looking for the umbrella. He knew he was. He didn't say so, and Ros didn't ask, though she must have seen him working backwards through the years with his finger on the column.

He found it in 1991. October. *Umbrella, green, carved handle (duck). 8.14 from Dunford, carriage 3. Handed in by guard.* And in the DISPOSAL column, three days later: *Collected*, and a signature, careful and round, the kind of handwriting they taught you once and then stopped teaching: *M. Abernathy.*

"M," Ivo said out loud.

Ros was at the hatch, serving a woman who'd lost a laptop. When the woman had gone she came and stood behind him and looked at the page for a long time.

"Margaret," she said. "His wife. She used to come in with him, years ago, when I'd only just started. She did the talking. He just stood there holding things." She closed the ledger gently, the way you'd close a door on someone sleeping. "She died at Easter."

"So he got it back. In 1991. It's not even lost."

"It's lost now," said Ros. "Somewhere."

---

Ivo thought about it all that week, at night, in the flat, while his mum's key didn't turn in the lock until six in the morning. He thought about the woman with the round handwriting coming in on a Thursday to pick up her husband's umbrella because he'd left it on the train again, and saying *honestly*, and his name, whatever it was, and Mr Abernathy just standing there, holding it.

On Saturday he took the bus into town and went round the charity shops. It took four. In the fourth one, in a stand by the door between a golf umbrella and a broken parasol, there was a green umbrella with a wooden handle carved into a bird's head. It wasn't a duck. It was more like a heron, or something that couldn't decide. It cost three pounds.

He brought it to work on Monday in a carrier bag and put it under the counter, and on Wednesday afternoon Ros found it.

She took it out and opened it, which you weren't supposed to do indoors, and looked up at the inside of it for a while, at the light coming green through the fabric.

"It's not a duck," she said.

"It's nearly a duck."

"Ivo." She closed it. "You don't get to decide what he needs."

"Neither do you. You decide every week. You decide he needs tea and a lie."

He thought she would be angry, the quiet kind. Instead she sat down on the stool behind the counter, still holding the umbrella across her knees, and she looked older than she had in June.

"Maybe," she said. "Maybe that's what I do." She was quiet for a while. "What happens after you give it to him? Have you thought about that bit? Does he stop coming? Does he come back next week and say he's lost it again? Does he look at it and know it's not his, and know that we know?"

"I don't know."

"No," she said. "Me neither. That's why I make the tea."

She handed the umbrella back to him. She didn't tell him to take it home.

---

On Thursday Ivo got there early and put the umbrella under the counter, handle out, where he could reach it without looking. He had a sentence ready. He'd practised it on the bus: *Someone handed this in. Is it yours?* That wasn't even a lie. He'd handed it in. He was someone.

At eleven the hatch was empty.

At half past, a man came in about a bike lock, and a girl about a phone that turned out to be in her other pocket.

At twelve Ros put the kettle on anyway and made two cups and drank one of them herself, and left the other on the counter by the hatch until it went cold and grew a skin.

He didn't come the Thursday after, either. Ros rang the station manager, who rang someone else, who didn't ring back. On the third Thursday she stopped making the second cup. Ivo noticed she still stood by the hatch at eleven, for a minute, with her hand flat on the counter, and then went back to the phones.

Nobody told them anything. That was the thing about the lost property office, Ros said, on his last day. You only ever saw half. People came in and lost something, or found it, and went back out into the rest of their lives, and you never heard the end.

"That's rubbish," said Ivo.

"It is," she agreed. "It's a rubbish job. I love it."

---

Before he left, while Ros was out the back, he took the green book down from the counter and opened it to the day's page. He took the umbrella out of the carrier bag and stood it in the bin with the others, at the front, where you'd see it first.

Then he wrote in the ledger, in his best handwriting, which wasn't very good:

*Umbrella, green, carved handle (nearly a duck). Handed in by: staff.*

He looked at the DISPOSAL column for a long time. You were supposed to leave it blank until something happened. In the end he wrote, in pencil, so it could be rubbed out:

*Holding for owner.*
