# Winter performance of air-source heat pumps retrofitted to pre-1930 solid-wall homes: results from 64 monitored dwellings in the Northern Vale

R. Achterberg, S. Mahmood, L. Feeney
Centre for Building Performance, University of Calder Vale

*Vale Journal of Housing and Energy*, Vol. 12, No. 3 (2026)

## Abstract

Older homes with solid masonry walls are widely assumed to be poorly suited to air-source heat pumps (ASHPs) because of their high heat loss. We monitored 64 pre-1930 solid-wall dwellings in the Northern Vale that had replaced gas boilers with ASHPs, over the 2025–26 heating season. Heat output, electricity input and indoor temperatures were recorded at two-minute intervals. The median seasonal performance factor (SPF H2) for space heating and hot water combined was 2.8 (interquartile range 2.4–3.1). Homes in which radiators had been enlarged or replaced as part of the installation performed better (median 3.0) than those which retained existing radiators (median 2.4). During the coldest period of the winter, 58 of 64 homes maintained living room temperatures of 19°C or above. Compared with modelled gas heating costs, median annual running costs were 9% higher on a standard single-rate electricity tariff and 12% lower on a time-of-use tariff. We conclude that solid-wall construction is not in itself a barrier to effective heat pump use, but that system design, in particular emitter sizing and flow temperature, has a substantial effect on performance and cost.

## 1. Introduction

Around a fifth of homes in the Northern Vale were built before 1930, most with solid brick or stone walls and no cavity to insulate. These homes typically have higher heat loss per square metre than later housing, and are often described by householders and some installers as "unsuitable" for heat pumps.

Heat pumps work most efficiently when they supply heat at low temperatures. A home with high heat loss either needs a higher flow temperature, which reduces efficiency, or larger radiators that can deliver the same heat at a lower temperature. Earlier field trials in mixed housing stock have reported average seasonal performance factors between 2.5 and 3.0, but included relatively few solid-wall homes, and few have reported indoor comfort alongside efficiency.

This study set out to answer three questions: how efficiently do ASHPs operate in pre-1930 solid-wall homes; can they keep these homes warm in cold weather; and how do running costs compare with the gas boilers they replaced?

## 2. Methods

**Recruitment.** Households were recruited between March and August 2025 through a public call publicised by the regional energy advice service and through three local installers. Eligible homes were built before 1930, had predominantly solid external walls, and had an ASHP installed between January 2024 and June 2025 replacing a gas boiler. Of 97 households who expressed interest, 71 were eligible and 64 completed the monitoring period. Seven withdrew or had equipment failures.

**Monitoring.** Each home was fitted with a heat meter on the heat pump output and an electricity sub-meter on the heat pump supply, recording at two-minute intervals. Temperature and humidity sensors were placed in the main living room and one bedroom. Monitoring ran from 1 October 2025 to 30 April 2026.

**Performance metric.** We report the seasonal performance factor at the H2 boundary (SPF H2), which is the total heat delivered for space heating and hot water divided by the electricity used by the heat pump unit, including its own pumps and controls but excluding any immersion heater or circulation pumps elsewhere in the home. An SPF of 3.0 means three units of heat were delivered for each unit of electricity.

**Running costs.** Because the original boilers had been removed, gas costs could not be measured directly. We modelled the gas that would have been needed to deliver the same measured heat using an assumed boiler efficiency of 85%, and applied unit prices and standing charges in force in January 2026. Electricity costs were calculated using each household's actual tariff. Twenty-three households were on a time-of-use tariff offering cheaper electricity during set hours; the remainder were on a standard single-rate tariff.

**Survey.** All households completed a questionnaire in May 2026 on comfort, noise, controls and overall satisfaction.

**Weather.** The 2025–26 heating season was approximately 7% milder than the 20-year regional average, measured in heating degree days. There were 11 days on which the daily mean outdoor temperature fell below 0°C, mostly in January.

## 3. Results

**Homes.** Of the 64 homes, 41 were mid- or end-terraced houses, 15 were semi-detached and 8 were detached. Median floor area was 96 m² (range 58–212 m²). 49 were owner-occupied and 15 privately rented. In 41 homes some insulation work (mainly loft insulation or draught-proofing) had been done in the five years before installation; only 6 had any form of external or internal wall insulation. Radiators had been enlarged or replaced in 39 homes and retained in 25.

**Efficiency.** The median SPF H2 was 2.8 (interquartile range 2.4–3.1; range 1.9–3.6).

| Group | n | Median SPF H2 |
|---|---|---|
| All homes | 64 | 2.8 |
| Radiators enlarged or replaced | 39 | 3.0 |
| Original radiators retained | 25 | 2.4 |
| Design flow temperature 50°C or below | 34 | 3.1 |
| Design flow temperature above 50°C | 30 | 2.5 |
| Some wall insulation | 6 | 2.9 |
| No wall insulation | 58 | 2.8 |

Performance for hot water alone was lower than for space heating, with a median of 2.1, reflecting the higher temperatures needed for stored hot water.

**Comfort.** On the 11 days below 0°C, 58 of 64 homes (91%) kept the living room at 19°C or above for at least 90% of occupied hours. Of the six that did not, four had heat pumps whose rated output was below the installer's own calculated heat loss for the property, and all six had retained their original radiators.

**Running costs.** On a standard single-rate tariff, the median annual cost of heating and hot water was 9% higher than modelled gas costs (range: 21% lower to 47% higher). Among the 23 households on a time-of-use tariff, the median cost was 12% lower than modelled gas costs (range: 31% lower to 18% higher). Cost outcomes were strongly linked to SPF: homes with an SPF of 3.0 or above were cheaper to run than gas on either tariff type in almost all cases.

**Installation costs.** The median installed cost was £12,400 (range £8,900–£21,300), including radiator work where carried out, before deduction of the £7,500 regional heat grant received by 58 households.

**Satisfaction.** 52 households (81%) said they were satisfied or very satisfied with their heat pump, 7 were neutral and 5 were dissatisfied. The most common complaints were about controls being hard to understand (14 households) and rooms taking longer to warm up (11). Four households reported noise problems, one of which had led to a complaint from a neighbour.

## 4. Discussion

Our results suggest that pre-1930 solid-wall homes can be heated effectively by air-source heat pumps. Median efficiency was within the range reported in earlier trials of mixed housing stock, and nine in ten homes stayed warm through the coldest days of the winter.

The factors most associated with good performance were design choices made at installation: enlarging radiators and designing for a lower flow temperature. Wall insulation made little measurable difference in this sample, though only six homes had it, so this finding should be treated with caution. The homes that struggled to stay warm were mostly those where the heat pump appeared to have been undersized relative to the installer's own heat loss estimate.

Running cost results depend heavily on electricity and gas prices, and on tariff. At the prices in force in January 2026, a household with an SPF below about 2.7 on a single-rate tariff was likely to pay more than for gas. Future changes in the relative price of gas and electricity would shift these results in either direction.

## 5. Limitations

Participants volunteered for the study and are likely to have been more motivated and better informed than typical households; many were recruited through installers, who may have referred their more successful jobs. Three installers carried out 71% of the installations, so results may reflect their practices rather than the market as a whole. Most participants were owner-occupiers. Monitoring covered a single heating season which was milder than average, and performance in a colder winter may be lower. Gas costs were modelled rather than measured, and the assumed boiler efficiency of 85% may overstate the efficiency of some older boilers that were replaced, which would make the comparison less favourable to heat pumps than it should be. Meter accuracy is estimated at ±5%.

## 6. Conclusions

Solid-wall construction alone should not rule out an air-source heat pump. The quality of the system design, especially radiator sizing, flow temperature and correct sizing of the heat pump itself, matters more to performance, comfort and running costs than the age or wall type of the house. Householders considering a heat pump in an older home should ask installers for a room-by-room heat loss calculation and should expect radiator upgrades to form part of a well-designed installation.
