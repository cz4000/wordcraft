# The Pigeon Loft

The loft had been Grandad's before the stroke, and now it was Dad's, and on Saturdays in June it was Nell's too, because there was nobody else to send.

"Clock's set," Dad said. He tapped the glass of the timing clock with one fingernail, as if it might be lying to him. "Liberated at quarter past seven. Two hundred and eleven miles. Wind in their faces all the way."

Nell knew what that meant. It meant a long wait. She sat on the upturned crate by the loft door, the one with SEED POTATOES stencilled on the side, and watched the sky over the allotments go from white to a thin, washed-out blue. Somewhere past the railway line a radio was playing. Mr Akhtar on plot nine was earthing up his leeks and pretending not to watch them.

Dad opened a can at ten o'clock. Nell didn't mean to notice. She had started noticing things since April, the way you notice a tooth that has begun to hurt: the cans in the recycling, how many, which day. Mum had gone to stay with Auntie Gail in Carrow "for a bit, just to get my head straight," and a bit had turned out to be nine weeks. She phoned on Sundays. Nell talked to her about school. Neither of them talked about anything else, which Nell had come to think of as a kind of agreement, though nobody had agreed to it out loud.

"Which one'll be first?" Dad said.

"The Professor."

"The Professor," Dad agreed. "Old devil. He'll come in like he's got a train to catch."

Nell wasn't really waiting for the Professor. She was waiting for Margot. Margot was a blue chequer hen, small and neat, with a white flight feather on her left wing like a mistake someone had decided to keep. On the race sheet she was 41. Dad said you shouldn't name racing birds, because you'd only end up upset, but Grandad had named every bird he ever had, and Nell had named Margot when she was a squab that fitted in two cupped hands, and Dad had not said anything about it then.

At ten to twelve a speck came over the poplars, and dropped, and was the Professor, exactly as promised, coming in so fast he overshot the landing board and had to go round again. Dad was up before Nell had finished saying "There." He coaxed the bird into the trap with a rattle of the seed tin, slid the rubber race ring off its leg, dropped it into the thimble and pushed the thimble into the clock. The clock made a sound like a bone clicking into place.

"Twelve minutes ahead of last year," Dad said, and for a second his face was the old face, the one from before April.

The others came in over the next hour, in ones and twos. Dad clocked each one and Nell wrote the times in the book in her best writing. Seven birds. Eight. Nine. Not Margot.

"She's a hen," Dad said. "Hens are slower. They've got more sense than to kill themselves for a rubber ring."

It was just after one when the young cock came in, one of this year's birds, a grizzle with no name. He was low over the allotments, flying hard, and Nell had just stood up to watch him drop when something came off the top of the poplars the way a stone comes off a wall.

She didn't see it hit. One moment there was the pigeon and the next there was a burst of grey, like someone shaking out a pillow, and the hawk was going away low over the bean rows with something hanging from its feet. Feathers came down on the path and on the potato haulms and on Nell. One stuck to her lip. She spat it off and then wished she hadn't, because it seemed rude, somehow, to spit out part of a bird.

"Shit," Dad said. Then, much louder, "*Shit*." He threw the can. It hit the side of the shed and fizzed. Then he kicked the water bucket, and the bucket went over and rolled across the path in a long, stupid curve, and the water ran into the dust.

Nell stood very still. Mr Akhtar had stopped hoeing.

Dad stood with his hands on his hips and his back to her, breathing. After a while he said, without turning round, "Sorry, love."

"It's all right."

"It's not all right. I shouldn't—" He picked the bucket up. "Sparrowhawk. They nest in the cemetery. Your grandad used to go mad." He looked at the feathers on the path. There was a smear of something darker on one of the flagstones and he scuffed it with his boot until it was gone. "Don't tell your mum I said shit."

"I won't," Nell said, and then they both thought about when she might next get the chance, and neither of them said that either.

---

By six Margot still wasn't home. Dad stopped looking at the sky and started doing jobs: scraping the perches, refilling the drinkers, things that didn't need doing. Nell sat on the crate with her knees up and watched the poplars.

"She might have dropped in somewhere," Dad said. "A bird can stop on a roof for a night and come home Monday like nothing's happened. Your grandad had a hen come back after nine days once. Nine days. Walked in the door like she'd been to the shops."

"Do you think the hawk got her?"

"I think she's a sensible hen who's stopped for the night."

"But do you *think*—"

"I don't know, Nell." He said it gently. "I don't know. That's the honest answer."

He didn't lock the trap when they left. Nell watched him not do it. Grandad had a rule, Dad said, that you never shut the trap on a bird that was still out. You left it so she could get in.

On the walk home, past the garages, Nell asked the question she had been carrying around for nine weeks, like a stone in her shoe.

"Is Mum coming back?"

Dad walked a few steps before he answered. "That's up to your mum, love."

"Is it up to you as well?"

He stopped then, under the streetlight that didn't work, and looked at her like he was seeing her from a long way off and she'd got bigger on the way.

"Yeah," he said. "Some of it is. Some of it's up to me."

He didn't say anything else, and she didn't ask. But that night, when she went down for a glass of water, he was sitting at the kitchen table with a mug of tea, not a can, and the radio on low, and he looked up and said "All right?" and she said "All right," and went back up to bed.

---

On Sunday Margot didn't come. Mum phoned, and Nell talked about school.

On Monday Nell went to the loft before school, running the long way round so she wouldn't have to pass the cemetery, and the landing board was empty, and the trap swung when she touched it.

On Tuesday she nearly didn't go. She stood at the allotment gate with her school bag on and thought, *if I don't look, she might still be coming*. Then she went anyway, because that was a stupid thing to think, and because Grandad would have gone.

Margot was sitting on the landing board.

She looked wrong. Thinner, her feathers lifted and untidy, as if she had been put together in a hurry. There was a rust-coloured line across her chest where something had opened the skin, and the white flight feather on her left wing was gone, and two others with it. But she was there, and when Nell said her name, very quietly, she turned her head and looked at Nell with one orange eye as if to say *well, you took your time*.

Nell picked her up the way Grandad had shown her, wings folded under her fingers, and the bird's heart went against her palms like something knocking to be let in.

She rang Dad from the loft. He came in his work clothes, with his hair not brushed, and he didn't say anything at all for a while. He found a cardboard box and put straw in it and a dish of water and a handful of seed, and he looked at the cut on Margot's chest and said it would heal, it wasn't deep, she'd been lucky. Then he said "Silly cow," very softly, to the bird, and Nell saw that his eyes were wet, and she looked away to give him the privacy of it.

"Nine days," Dad said eventually. "Your grandad's hen. I always thought he'd made that up."

"Three days isn't nine."

"No. But it's not nothing."

Nell was late for school. Dad wrote her a note that said *Family matter* and underlined it twice.

---

That night her phone buzzed on the bed beside her and it said MUM, and it wasn't Sunday.

Nell looked at it for three rings. Then she took it out to the landing, and sat on the top stair in the dark, and answered.

"Hi," Mum said. Her voice sounded careful, the way you carry something full to the brim. "I just wanted to hear you. Is that all right?"

"Margot came back," Nell said.

There was a pause. "Margot?"

"My hen. She was out for three days. Everyone thought the hawk got her. But she came back on her own."

Another pause, longer, and Nell could hear something on the other end of the line that might have been the television or might not.

"Tell me," Mum said. "Tell me all of it."

So Nell did. And downstairs, she could hear Dad moving about the kitchen, and the radio, and the kettle starting to boil, and she thought about the trap on the loft, still unlocked, swinging a little in the wind, and left it that way in her head, just in case.
