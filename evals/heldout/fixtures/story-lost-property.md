# The Duck-Handled Umbrella

The lost property office at Harrow Cross station was underground, at the end of a tiled corridor that smelled faintly of wet coats. Marguerite Bell had worked there for nineteen years. In that time she had logged eleven thousand umbrellas, four hundred and six pairs of spectacles, a prosthetic leg, a wedding dress still in its bag, two urns of ashes (both collected, eventually) and a tortoise, which had not been lost property in the strict sense but had needed somewhere to go.

She liked the work because it was orderly. Things came in, things were written down, and things either went home or, after ninety days, went to auction. Most people who came to the hatch were flustered and grateful. She was good with flustered people. She had a way of saying "Let's have a look" that made them breathe out.

The boy first came on a Saturday in February. He was about eleven, in a school coat that was too big for him, and he had to stand on the step to see over the counter.

"Has anyone handed in an umbrella?" he said. "It's blue. The handle's a duck."

"A duck?"

"A wooden duck. The head is the handle. It's got a yellow beak."

Marguerite checked the ledger, then the racks. There were forty-one umbrellas from that week alone, but none of them blue with a duck. She told him so, and took his name, Owen, and a phone number, and said she'd ring if it turned up.

"When did you lose it?" she asked.

He thought about it. "October."

She didn't tell him that anything from October would have gone to auction by now. He looked as though he already knew.

The next Saturday he was back. Same coat, same question. Marguerite checked again, because it cost nothing to check, and again there was nothing.

By the fourth Saturday, her colleague Desmond had started calling him "the Duck Boy".

"You know it's not coming back," Desmond said, when the boy had gone. Desmond had worked there for three years and considered himself a realist. "He's wasting your time."

"It's my time," Marguerite said.

"It's the railway's time, technically."

She ignored him. But she started paying attention. Owen always came at twenty past eleven, which meant he was on the 10:52 from Wenlow, the small town at the end of the line. He always came alone. He never asked about anything except the umbrella, and he never seemed upset when she said no. He'd nod, as if confirming something, and say thank you, and go back up the corridor.

On the sixth Saturday, she asked him about it.

"Whose umbrella was it?"

"My nan's."

"Ah," said Marguerite, and waited, because waiting was usually better than asking.

"She used to bring me into the city on Saturdays," Owen said. "We'd go to the museum or the market. She always brought the duck umbrella, even if it was sunny. She said it was in case." He rubbed the edge of the counter with his thumb. "We left it on the train. The last time."

Marguerite understood then what "the last time" meant, and she didn't ask anything else.

That evening, at home, she found she couldn't stop thinking about it. She lived alone in a flat above a dry cleaner's, with a cat who tolerated her and a radio that was always on. She ate her supper at the window and watched people go past with their umbrellas up, and she counted the ones she might have logged. It was a habit she had never told anyone about.

Her own mother had died eight years before. Marguerite had cleared the house herself, over three weekends, and had given almost everything away. She had kept a sewing tin and a pair of gardening gloves, and she couldn't have said why those and not the good china. Later she had wished she'd kept the coat her mother wore every winter, a brown wool thing with a torn lining, but by then it had gone to a charity shop and been sold to a stranger. She had once caught herself, in the street, looking for it on other women.

On Monday morning, Desmond found her at the computer before the hatch opened.

"What are you doing?"

"Looking for a duck," said Marguerite.

---

That week she did something she had never done in nineteen years. She went through the auction records.

The railway's unclaimed items were sold in bulk lots every quarter by a firm in an industrial park on the edge of the city. Umbrellas were sold by the crate. Most crates went to market traders, who resold them for a few pounds each on wet days. The records were not detailed. "Umbrellas, assorted, approx. 120" was a typical line.

But the December lot had not yet been collected. It was still in the firm's warehouse, waiting for a buyer who had not paid. Marguerite rang them, explained who she was, and on her day off she took two buses to the industrial park and spent three hours going through five crates of other people's umbrellas in an unheated shed.

It was slow, cold work. Most of the umbrellas were black. Many were broken. She found a golf umbrella with a hotel's name on it, a child's umbrella shaped like a frog, and a beautiful old one with a silver collar and a cane handle that she felt guilty for not being looking for. The man from the firm brought her a mug of tea halfway through and asked what she'd lost. She said it wasn't hers, and he nodded as if that happened all the time.

In the fourth crate, near the bottom, she found it. Blue canvas, faded at the folds. A wooden duck's head for a handle, with a yellow beak.

She paid fourteen pounds for the whole crate, because the firm would not split it, and arranged for the rest to be given to a charity shop. She carried the duck umbrella home on the bus with both hands, as if it might fly off.

---

On Saturday, at twenty past eleven, Owen stepped up onto the step.

"Has anyone handed in—"

Marguerite put the umbrella on the counter.

She had imagined this moment several times during the week. She had imagined him gasping, or crying, or running his hands over it. She had imagined telling Desmond about it afterwards.

Owen looked at the umbrella for a long time. Then he picked it up, turned the duck's head towards him, and looked at the beak.

"It's not hers," he said.

Marguerite felt something drop in her chest. "Are you sure? Blue, with a duck—"

"Hers had a chip out of the beak. Here." He touched the very tip. "She dropped it on the steps at the museum. She said the duck had been in a fight." He put the umbrella back on the counter, carefully, and pushed it a little towards her. "This one's nice, though."

"I'm sorry," Marguerite said. "I thought—"

"It's okay." He said it kindly, the way an adult would. Then he looked at the counter, not at her, and said, "I knew it wouldn't be here. I knew after the first time."

"Then why do you keep coming?"

He shrugged, a big, awkward shrug that went all the way up to his ears.

"Mum lets me get the train on my own if I'm going somewhere specific," he said. "If I say I'm just going into town, she says no. But if I say I'm going to the lost property office to ask about Nan's umbrella, she says yes." He paused. "And then I go to the museum. And the market. Same as we used to. I just come here first so it's not a lie."

Marguerite didn't say anything for a moment. Behind her, Desmond had stopped pretending to sort the gloves.

"Does your mum know that's what you do?"

"I think she knows," Owen said. "She gives me money for a hot chocolate. Nan always got me a hot chocolate."

---

After he had gone, Desmond came over and looked at the umbrella.

"Fourteen quid," he said. "For the wrong duck."

"Yes."

"And a whole day off."

"Yes."

He was quiet for a while. Then he said, "You're going to keep telling him you're still looking, aren't you."

Marguerite picked up the umbrella and put it under the counter, on the shelf where she kept her flask and her reading glasses.

"I am still looking," she said.

---

The next Saturday, Owen came at twenty past eleven.

"Has anyone handed in an umbrella? Blue, with a duck handle?"

Marguerite made a show of checking the ledger. She went to the racks. She came back.

"Not this week," she said. "But we'll keep looking. What's on at the museum?"

"Fossils," Owen said. "There's a new ichthyosaur."

"I don't know what that is."

"It's like a dolphin but a lizard. Sort of." He frowned. "I'll tell you next week."

"Do," said Marguerite.

He went back up the tiled corridor, and she listened to his footsteps until they were lost in the noise of the trains.

It rained that evening. When Marguerite left at six, she took the blue umbrella from under the counter and opened it on the station steps. The duck's head fitted her hand well. A man waiting for a taxi looked at it and smiled, and she found that she was smiling back.

She kept it after that. It was, after all, unclaimed.
