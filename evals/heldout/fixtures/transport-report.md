# Moving Corrinford: The City Transport Plan 2027–2036

## Consultation Findings and Recommended Programme

*Report to the Cabinet Member for Environment and Transport. Prepared by the Transport Strategy Team, Corrinford City Council. Version 1.3, October 2026.*

---

### 1. Introduction

This report sets out the findings of the public consultation on the draft City Transport Plan, which ran from 3 March to 26 May 2026, and recommends a programme of work for the ten years from April 2027. It replaces the Local Transport Strategy adopted in 2016, most of whose targets have either been met, overtaken by events or quietly dropped.

The draft plan was built around three aims agreed by Cabinet in November 2025: make it easier to get around the city without a car; cut transport's contribution to the city's carbon emissions; and make streets safer, especially for children and older people. Those aims have not changed. What has changed, as a result of the consultation, is the order in which we propose to do things and the amount we propose to spend on some of them.

The report is in eight sections. Sections 2 and 3 describe where we are now and what people told us. Section 4 sets out the recommended programme. Sections 5 to 7 cover cost, timing and risk. Section 8 lists the decisions Cabinet is asked to make.

### 2. Where we are now

Corrinford has a population of about 312,000, with a further 140,000 people living in the surrounding towns and villages who travel into the city regularly for work, school, shopping or hospital appointments. The city centre sits in a river valley, which concentrates traffic onto four main roads in and out: the Northway, the Ashby Road, the Quayside and the Lower Mill Road.

Car use remains high. In the 2025 travel survey, 58% of trips to work were made by car (down from 64% in 2015), 17% by bus, 11% on foot, 6% by bike, 4% by train and 4% by other means, including working from home on a given day being recorded inconsistently. The fall in car commuting is real but slower than the 2016 strategy hoped; its target was 50% by 2025.

Bus use has not recovered from the pandemic years. Passenger journeys were 21.4 million in 2019 and 17.9 million in 2025, a fall of about 16%. The two operators, Valley Buses and Corrin Coaches, both report that journeys are slower and less reliable than ten years ago. Average bus speed on the Northway corridor at the evening peak is now 9.2 miles per hour, against 11.8 in 2015. Slower buses need more vehicles and drivers to run the same timetable, which pushes up costs and has led to cuts on evening and Sunday services.

Cycling has grown, from a low base. Counts at the eight permanent cycle counters rose by 34% between 2019 and 2025, with the biggest rise on the riverside path, which was resurfaced and lit in 2022. The city has 41 miles of cycle route, but only 9 miles of that is physically separated from traffic, and the network has several well-known gaps, most notably the crossing of the ring road at Fennel Bridge.

Road safety has improved overall but not evenly. There were 96 people killed or seriously injured on the city's roads in 2025, down from 131 in 2016. However, the number of pedestrians seriously injured has barely moved, and 40% of child pedestrian casualties in the last five years happened within 400 metres of a school.

Transport accounts for about 31% of the city's carbon emissions, the largest single share, and it is the only sector where emissions have not fallen since 2016.

Air quality on the Quayside still breaches the legal limit for nitrogen dioxide at two monitoring points, although the number of days above the limit has halved since 2019.

### 3. What people told us

#### 3.1 Who responded

The consultation received 6,842 responses: 5,917 through the online survey, 611 on paper forms, 288 by email and letter, and 26 from organisations, including both bus operators, the hospital trust, the university, the Chamber of Commerce, three residents' associations and the Corrinford Cycling Campaign. In addition, around 1,400 people attended 22 drop-in events held in libraries, community centres, the covered market and, on two Saturdays, a stall at the Riverside retail park. The team also ran four sessions with secondary school pupils and two with residents of sheltered housing.

Respondents were older than the city as a whole: 46% were over 55, compared with 31% of adults in the city. Car drivers were also somewhat over-represented. We have weighted the survey results by age and area, and the weighted and unweighted results differ by no more than four percentage points on any of the main questions, so the findings below use the unweighted figures for simplicity.

#### 3.2 Overall support

Asked whether they supported the overall aims of the plan, 71% agreed or strongly agreed, 14% were neutral and 15% disagreed or strongly disagreed. Support was higher among under-35s (82%) and lower among people living in the outer estates and villages (59%).

#### 3.3 Buses

Better buses were the single most popular theme. Asked to pick their top three priorities from a list of twelve, 63% of respondents chose "more reliable buses" and 48% chose "more frequent buses". Free-text comments about buses were overwhelmingly about reliability rather than fares: people described waiting for buses that never came, real-time displays that were wrong, and services that were cut without warning. Several hundred comments mentioned the withdrawal of the 7 and 7A evening services in 2024, which residents of Hartsfield said had left them unable to get home from late shifts.

The draft plan's proposal for bus lanes on the Northway and Ashby Road was supported by 61% and opposed by 27%. Opposition came mostly from people who drive on those roads daily and from some businesses on the Ashby Road, who were worried about losing loading space and on-street parking outside their shops.

#### 3.4 The city centre

The draft proposed closing Bridge Street and the eastern half of Market Street to through traffic. This was the most divisive proposal in the plan. Overall, 52% supported it and 38% opposed it. Market traders and some shop owners were strongly opposed, citing fears about deliveries and passing trade; the Chamber of Commerce submitted a detailed response asking for a trial rather than a permanent scheme. Residents of the centre and visitors who arrive by bus or on foot were strongly in favour. Disability groups were split: some welcomed calmer streets, while others stressed that blue badge holders need to be able to park close to shops and the covered market.

#### 3.5 Walking and cycling

Safer routes to school were chosen as a top-three priority by 44% of respondents, more than any other walking or cycling measure. There was strong support (74%) for "school streets", where roads outside a school are closed to motor traffic at drop-off and pick-up times.

A separated cycle network was a top-three priority for 29% of respondents. Many comments said people would cycle if routes felt safe, and the Fennel Bridge crossing was mentioned by name more than 600 times. A smaller number of respondents, mostly older, raised concerns about cyclists on shared paths and asked for clearer separation between people walking and people cycling.

#### 3.6 Charging the cost of parking

The draft included a workplace parking levy, a charge on employers for each staff parking space above a threshold, with the money ring-fenced for transport. This was opposed by 49% and supported by 33%. The Chamber of Commerce and the hospital trust both opposed it. The hospital trust pointed out that many of its staff work shifts when buses do not run. The university supported it in principle.

#### 3.7 Other points raised

Respondents raised many other issues, including potholes (mentioned in about 1,100 comments, although road maintenance is funded separately and outside the scope of this plan), the poor state of the bus station toilets, the colour of the new bus shelters in the Westgate area (several dozen people disliked the dark green, which they said was hard to see at night), dogs on buses, e-scooters, and the lack of a direct bus to the Riverside retail park on Sundays. A proposal to rename the bus station "Corrinford Interchange" drew 214 comments, most of them against.

### 4. The recommended programme

In light of the consultation, the team recommends five programmes. The main changes from the draft are that bus reliability moves to the front of the plan, the city centre scheme becomes an 18-month trial, and the workplace parking levy is deferred.

**Programme 1: Bus Reliability.** Bus lanes and bus priority at traffic signals on the Northway and Ashby Road corridors, with bus lanes operating at peak hours only on the Ashby Road and loading bays retained outside shops between 10am and 3pm. A new agreement with both operators to restore the 7 and 7A evening services and add evening frequency on the four main corridors, funded in part by the operators' savings from faster journeys. Replacement of the real-time information system, which is 14 years old and no longer supported by its supplier.

**Programme 2: City Centre Trial.** An 18-month trial closing Bridge Street and eastern Market Street to through traffic, using planters and temporary materials, with access maintained for deliveries before 10am, blue badge holders, taxis and buses. A full evaluation, including footfall counts and a survey of traders, before any decision on a permanent scheme.

**Programme 3: Safer Streets for Schools.** School streets at up to 30 primary schools over the first five years, starting with the 12 schools where parents and staff have already asked for one. Pedestrian crossing improvements within 400 metres of every school with a casualty record.

**Programme 4: Connected Cycle Network.** A new bridge or underpass at Fennel Bridge, followed by separated routes on the three corridors where gaps are worst. Clearer separation on shared paths, especially on the riverside route.

**Programme 5: Cleaner Vehicles.** Public electric vehicle charging in residential streets without off-street parking, and support for both bus operators to bid for national funding for electric buses.

The workplace parking levy would be reconsidered in 2029 once bus improvements are in place, so that people have a realistic alternative before any charge is introduced.

### 5. Cost and funding

The recommended programme would cost an estimated £184 million over ten years at today's prices. Of this, about £121 million is already secured or highly likely through the national regional transport settlement and existing council budgets. Programme 1 accounts for £58 million, Programme 2 for £4.5 million (trial phase only), Programme 3 for £16 million, Programme 4 for £71 million (of which the Fennel Bridge crossing alone is £38 million) and Programme 5 for £22 million. The remaining £12.5 million covers design, monitoring, staff and contingency.

The funding gap of about £63 million is mainly in Programme 4. Deferring the workplace parking levy removes an estimated £6 million a year in income that the draft plan had counted on, which is why the Fennel Bridge crossing is now phased later in the decade, subject to a bid to the national active travel fund in 2028.

### 6. Timing

Year 1 (2027–28) would deliver the Ashby Road bus lanes, the restored evening services, the start of the city centre trial, the first 12 school streets and the replacement of the real-time information system. Years 2 to 4 would deliver the Northway bus lanes, the evaluation of the city centre trial, further school streets and the design of the Fennel Bridge crossing. Construction at Fennel Bridge would start no earlier than 2030.

### 7. Risks

The main risks are: that operators do not commit to restoring services, in which case the council may need to fund them directly; that construction costs continue to rise faster than inflation; that the city centre trial is judged too early, before travel habits have had time to settle; and that national funding rules change. The plan also depends on recruiting two traffic engineers, posts that have been vacant for over a year.

### 8. Decisions requested

Cabinet is asked to: approve the five programmes for further development; approve the 18-month city centre trial to begin in spring 2027; agree to defer the workplace parking levy until 2029; and note that a separate report on bus station improvements, including the naming question, will follow in the new year.
