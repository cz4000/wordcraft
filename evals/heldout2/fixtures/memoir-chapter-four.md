# Chapter Four: Ninety-Nines

The summer I was thirteen my mother put me on the coach to Sallowby with a holdall, a packed lunch and a five-pound note folded into the lining of my sandal, and told me it would only be until things were sorted out at home. She didn't say what things. I knew anyway. My father had been sleeping in the box room since Easter, and the house had gone quiet in the way a house goes quiet when two people have stopped arguing because they have run out of things to argue about.

My grandmother Nell met me off the coach in her van.

I should explain about the van. It was an old coachbuilt van, cream and raspberry, with NELL'S on the side in letters my grandfather had painted by hand the year before he died, and underneath, in smaller letters that had faded almost to nothing, *Ices – Lollies – Ninety-Nines*. It had a chime that was supposed to play "Greensleeves" but had lost two notes somewhere in the 1960s, so it played something that sounded like Greensleeves after a long illness. Every child in Sallowby knew it. When it came round the corner of the Brookfield estate at four o'clock they came running out of the houses as if someone had rung a fire bell.

"You'll be working," Nell said, before I'd even got the holdall in the back. "I hope you can count."

I could count. What I couldn't do, it turned out, was count fast with a queue of fourteen children waving coins at me and the soft-serve machine dripping on my wrist. For the first week I got everything wrong. I gave a boy change from a pound when he'd given me fifty pence. I put a flake in a cone that hadn't been paid for a flake. I dropped a whole tub of raspberry ripple on the floor of the van and Nell said nothing at all, just handed me a cloth, which was worse than shouting.

But by the second week I was good. By the third I was better than good. I could take an order of three ninety-nines, a strawberry split and two ice pops from a woman with a pushchair, add it up in my head, and have the change in her hand before she'd finished checking her purse. Nell started letting me do the money altogether. "My eyes," she said, tapping the side of her head. "Numbers swim about." She did the cones and the chat. She was very good at the chat. She knew every family on every estate, whose dad was out of work, whose nan had been poorly, which children were allowed a flake and which weren't, and she gave things away constantly, an extra wafer here, a free lolly there, in a way that would have driven my mother to distraction.

There was another van that summer. The Castellano brothers had come up from somewhere down the coast with a brand-new van, all chrome and blue paint, with a chime that played "O Sole Mio" perfectly, every note in place. They started doing the Brookfield estate at quarter to four, fifteen minutes before us. Nell called them "the Italians" although the younger one, Declan, had a Sallowby accent as broad as hers and had been at school with my cousin. Declan was sixteen and did the serving hatch on their van. He had a way of leaning out of it on his elbows that I thought, at thirteen, was the most sophisticated thing I had ever seen.

I am not going to pretend I didn't spend a great deal of that summer thinking about Declan Castellano. I did. Once, when both vans were parked up on the seafront, he walked over and asked if we had any change for a five, and I gave it to him so fast I dropped two of the coins in the gutter, and he laughed, not unkindly, and picked them up, and said "Ta, Ruthie," and I didn't sleep that night. That was more or less the whole of it. He never said anything else to me that I remember. I mention him because he was the thing I thought the summer was about, at the time, and he wasn't.

Nell didn't mind the Castellanos, or said she didn't. "Plenty of children," she said. "Plenty of heat." But the takings were down and I knew it because I was the one counting them. Every night we sat at her kitchen table with the cash bag, and I stacked the coins in towers of ten and wrote the total in the red notebook, and she watched me do it. She never once checked my sums.

The letter came in the second week of August.

It was a Tuesday, and it had been raining since lunchtime, the kind of grey sideways rain that empties a seaside town in twenty minutes. We'd given up and come home early. There was a brown envelope on the mat with the council's crest on it, and Nell picked it up and put it on the dresser behind the clock without opening it, and made the tea.

After tea she took it down again. She turned it over a few times. Then she held it out to me.

"Read us that, love," she said. "I've left my glasses in the van."

I remember that I had to go and look for the light switch, because it had got so dark with the rain, and that when I came back she was sitting very straight in her chair with her hands in her lap, the way people sit in a doctor's waiting room.

The letter said that the council was reviewing the street-trading pitches on the seafront for the following year, and that existing holders must reapply on the enclosed form by Friday the 22nd, or their pitch would be offered to other applicants. It was two pages long and the form was four. I read it all out, and when I got to the end Nell said, "Well, that's that, then," and I didn't understand what she meant.

"It's just a form," I said. "I'll help you fill it in."

She looked at me for a long moment. Then she said, "Your grandad always did the forms."

I had seen her glasses that afternoon. They were in the pocket of her housecoat, hanging on the back of the kitchen door. And in the minute or so after she said that, a whole lot of other things arrived in my head at once, the way a stack of coins falls over: that she'd never checked the red notebook; that she ordered from the wholesaler by telephone and had me read out the invoice when it came "because of her eyes"; that she had never, in three weeks, read a newspaper or a magazine or the back of a cereal packet in front of me; that the chalkboard on the side of the van was in my grandfather's handwriting and had not been changed, even though the prices on it were wrong, since before he died.

I didn't say any of it. I don't know how I knew not to. I think I was more frightened than I'd ever been, not of anything that might happen, but of the look on her face, which was the look of someone standing very still in case the ground gave way.

"I'm good at forms," I said. "We do them at school."

We filled it in together at the kitchen table, with the rain still coming down. I read her each question and she told me the answer and I wrote it in my best handwriting, which was not very good. Name. Address. Vehicle registration. Years of trading: she said thirty-one, then corrected herself to thirty-two. At the bottom there was a box for her signature. She went to her handbag and took out a little card, about the size of a bus pass, and laid it on the table beside the form. On it, in my grandfather's writing, was her name: *Eleanor M. Haworth*. She copied it, slowly, letter by letter, her tongue between her teeth like a child, and it came out shaky but recognisable, and she put the card back in her bag and zipped it up.

Neither of us mentioned it again, then or ever.

I posted the form on the Wednesday morning, and she got her pitch back for the following year, and in the last week of August the sun came out and we had the best three days' trading of the whole summer, and on the very last day Declan Castellano waved at me from his hatch and I found that I didn't much care.

My mother came to fetch me on the first of September. Things at home had been sorted out, which meant my father had moved to a flat above a carpet shop in town and I would see him on Sundays. On the drive back I told my mother about the van and the ninety-nines and the Castellanos, and she laughed in the right places, and I didn't tell her about the form. I've never told anyone, until now.

Nell sold the van the following spring. She told the family it was her knees. She was sixty-seven, and her knees were bad, so it may well have been true. But I have always wondered whether she sold it because there was no one left to do the money, and because she couldn't face asking anyone else to read her the letters.

When she died, eleven years later, my aunt and I cleared her bungalow. In the zip pocket of her handbag, worn soft at the corners, was the little card with her name on it. My aunt turned it over and said, "What on earth's this?"

"No idea," I said, and put it in my coat.

I have it still. It's on the desk in front of me as I write this.
