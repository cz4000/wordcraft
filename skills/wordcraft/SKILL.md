# Wordcraft

Good writing is one person talking to another, clearly. The reader is busy, wasn't inside your head while you worked, and should understand you the first time. Everything below serves that reader.

## How to write it

1. **Start where the reader is.** Before any detail, say in a sentence or two what this is about and why it matters to them: what you set out to do, and where it stands now.
2. **Say what each thing is for, and so what.** For every change, finding or name, tell the reader its purpose and what it means for them, and why you chose it when there was a choice. A name they haven't seen (a file, function, flag or package) comes after the plain description, in brackets. Names they already know need no explanation.
3. **Cut the padding, never the substance.** Remove repetition, filler and throat-clearing. Keep every fact, caveat, step and leftover the reader would want. A chat question gets a chat-sized answer; a report gets more.
4. **Match the shape to the content.** Use paragraphs for reasoning. Use a list, or a few short headed groups, for separate items: changes, options, status, next steps. Add a small diagram or example when it shows something faster than words. Don't chop one line of reasoning into bold-labelled fragments.
5. **Write to this person about this situation.** Use the specifics you were given and answer what the other person actually said. Stock phrases and pleasantries make writing generic. Plain beats breezy, and warm beats cute.
6. **Stay true to the material.** Every fact comes from the request or what came with it. If you had to guess something the reader must check, like a date or a number, say so once, in one line. Otherwise don't comment on your own draft.
7. **Let the words carry the emphasis.** Leave out decorative emoji. Bold only what a skimmer must not miss.
8. **When you've made your point, stop.** End on the last useful sentence, not on a recap or an offer of more help.

Keep what already works: concrete numbers and examples, an honest line about what you couldn't check, and a voice that sounds like a person.

## Before you send

Reread the draft as the reader, who has only what's on the page:

- Do they know from the opening what this is about and why it matters?
- Is there anything whose purpose they'd have to guess?
- Could they act on it after one read?
- Is every fact in it true to what you were given?
